My research examines how public policy and economic activity interact across places, with a focus on local labor markets, spatial equilibrium, and political behavior.

Job Market Paper

The Effect of Public Sector Relocations on Regional Development in Germany

Job Market Paper

As regional economic disparities within countries grow, governments are increasingly experimenting with public employment reallocation as a place-based policy. Using novel data on 60 agency relocations from 2015–2025, I estimate employment and population effects for receiving and sending municipalities and use the results to discipline a quantitative spatial model. Relocations increased private employment shares by up to 2.3% (1.3 percentage points), reduced unemployment by up to 11.9% (0.33 percentage points), and raised population by 1.6%, implying a public-to-private jobs multiplier of 1.08 in receiving locations. Sending locations also see increased private-sector employment, rationalized in the model by within-private-sector spillovers exceeding public-to-private-sector spillovers.

Working Papers

Do place-based policies matter at the poll?

With Luisa Doerr and Florian Dorn (ifo Munich)

This paper studies whether place-based policies affect electoral outcomes by exploiting a major public-sector relocation program in Germany, which relocated more than 60 agencies and 3,000 civil-service jobs to municipalities in structurally weaker regions of Bavaria between 2015 and 2025. Exploiting exogenous variation generated by the state’s rule-based allocation of public agencies, we find sizable electoral effects in treated municipalities. The incumbent governing party, CSU, gains just over 2 percentage points in state elections, while its coalition partner, the Free Voters, loses about 1.6 points. The program also reduces support for the AfD by roughly 0.5–1 point. Turnout falls by 0.72 percentage points in the first election following a relocation. In federal elections, we observe aligned but smaller shifts in vote shares.

Presented at: 18th ifo Workshop in Political Economy; European Public Choice Society Meeting 2025; Max Planck Institute for Tax Law and Public Finance (invited).

The Drivers of Far-Right Populism in Western Democracies

With Patrick Zwerschke

We create a dataset with more than 26 determinants of far-right populism in over 30 countries between 1980 and 2019. We use Bayesian Model Averaging to identify robust drivers and Bayesian Additive Regression Trees to examine nonlinearities. The results point to distinct relationships involving trade, imports from China, migrant stocks, refugee migration, and religiosity.

Presented at: Annual Congress of the Swiss Society of Economics and Statistics 2024; Aix-Marseille Research Seminar (invited).

A comment on Xu (2022) “Reshaping Global Trade: The Immediate and Long-Term Effects of Bank Failures”

With Guillaume Bérard (Luxembourg Institute of Socio-Economic Research) and Priyam Verma (Aix-Marseille Univ, CNRS, EHESS, AMSE)

We reproduce the paper’s findings on the effect of the 1866 banking crisis on trade, identify three issues—one of which slightly affects the reported main estimates—and test robustness to alternative weighting, spatial HAC standard errors, and a staggered difference-in-differences method allowing heterogeneous treatment effects. Overall, the main findings remain valid and robust.

NBER working paper

35 cents less for gasoline and 17 cents less for diesel — The fuel tax reduction was passed on to consumers — on average

With S. Syga · ifo Dresden berichtet 29 (2022), 13–18

This study evaluates a temporary reduction in fuel taxes in Germany amid the war in Ukraine. Using dynamic difference-in-differences and comparisons with similar policies in four other European countries, it finds that oil companies passed the tax reduction on to consumers, with the extent varying across countries and over time.

Work in Progress

The Growth–Affordability Trade-off in Zoning

With Gabriel Ahlfeldt, Jan Bakker, and Nina Gläser

We develop and quantify a spatial general equilibrium model to study growth–affordability trade-offs in land-use zoning. The model is disciplined using a unique micro-geographic dataset covering commercial and residential rents, employment, and wages for all of Germany. Counterfactual simulations show that constraining residential supply lowers office rents, fosters agglomeration and productivity, and raises output, but increases housing costs and harms low-skilled workers. Conversely, prioritizing residential development improves affordability and benefits low-skilled households, yet dampens productivity and wage growth. Our results highlight fundamental distributional trade-offs embedded in zoning policy and their implications for welfare and inequality.
