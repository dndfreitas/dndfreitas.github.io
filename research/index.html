The Effect of Public Sector Relocations on Regional Development in Germany

Combining newly collected relocation data with administrative employer–employee records, I estimate the consequences of public-job relocations for both the places gaining and losing employment. I then develop a quantitative spatial model in which public and private employment generate different productivity spillovers, linking the reduced-form evidence to the spatial mechanism.

The Potential of Public Employment Reallocation as a Place-Based Policy

In The Economics of Place-Based Policies, ed. Cécile Gaubert, Gordon H. Hanson, and David Neumark. Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 2026.

Increasing within-country disparities lead policymakers to deploy public employment reallocation as a place-based policy tool to support struggling regions. This paper surveys the economics literature on capital relocations, purpose-built capitals, and public agency decentralization programs, synthesizing their effects on population, employment, and GDP. I find that while relocating capital cities can spur employment, GDP, and population growth in receiving regions, they entail highly unpredictable costs (3–12 percent of GDP) and uncertain environmental outcomes. Decentralization programs yield positive short-run public-to-private employment multipliers (around 0.7) stemming from the non-traded sector, but the long-term effect on the traded sector remains ambiguous. Local initial conditions seem to matter more than ex-post spillovers in determining multiplier size. Although more evidence is needed, sending regions do not seem to be harmed when public jobs leave.

The Growth–Affordability Trade-off in Zoning

With Gabriel Ahlfeldt, Jan Bakker, and Nina Gläser

We develop and quantify a spatial general equilibrium model to study growth–affordability trade-offs in land-use zoning. The model is disciplined using a unique micro-geographic dataset covering commercial and residential rents, employment, and wages for all of Germany. Counterfactual simulations show that constraining residential supply lowers office rents, fosters agglomeration and productivity, and raises output, but increases housing costs and harms low-skilled workers. Conversely, prioritizing residential development improves affordability and benefits low-skilled households, yet dampens productivity and wage growth. Our results highlight fundamental distributional trade-offs embedded in zoning policy and their implications for welfare and inequality.

35 cents less for gasoline and 17 cents less for diesel — The fuel tax reduction was passed on to consumers — on average

With S. Syga · ifo Dresden berichtet 29 (2022), 13–18

We exploit Germany’s temporary 2022 fuel-tax reduction and comparable reforms in other European countries to estimate pass-through using dynamic difference-in-differences. The analysis shows how quickly and how fully temporary tax relief reaches consumers, and how pass-through varies across countries and over time.

Do place-based policies matter at the poll?

With Luisa Doerr and Florian Dorn (ifo Munich)

We exploit quasi-experimental variation from Bavaria’s rule-based public-agency relocations to ask whether place-based economic policy changes voting behavior. The design links a large regional-development intervention to electoral outcomes across treated municipalities and elections.

The Drivers of Far-Right Populism in Western Democracies

With Patrick Zwerschke

We assemble a cross-country dataset covering more than 26 candidate drivers of far-right populism in over 30 Western democracies and explicitly confront model uncertainty. Bayesian Model Averaging identifies robust determinants, while Bayesian Additive Regression Trees uncover nonlinearities that standard linear models can miss.

A comment on Xu (2022) “Reshaping Global Trade: The Immediate and Long-Term Effects of Bank Failures”

With Guillaume Bérard (Luxembourg Institute of Socio-Economic Research) and Priyam Verma (Aix-Marseille Univ, CNRS, EHESS, AMSE)

We reproduce Xu’s estimates of the long-run trade effects of the 1866 banking crisis, identify implementation issues, and reassess the results using alternative weighting, spatial-HAC inference, and staggered difference-in-differences. The paper’s central result remains robust.
